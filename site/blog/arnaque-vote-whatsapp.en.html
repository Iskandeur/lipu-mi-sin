“Vote for Sophie”: anatomy of a WhatsApp scam

One lunchtime, a message asks me to vote for friends' daughter in a ballet contest. That evening, two relatives of mine get almost the same one. Behind this little favour: a machine for stealing WhatsApp accounts, one that's been running for months.

Alexandre Pinoteau21.10.2026with help from Bounce

Two articles, one investigation. You can switch between them at any time.

If you only read this

  1. Don't click. A real contest never needs you to log in with WhatsApp in order to vote.
  2. Never type your number and then a WhatsApp code into a website. That code is the key to your account. Whoever receives it reads your conversations and speaks in your name.
  3. Go check right now in WhatsApp → Settings → Linked devices. Don't recognise a device? Log it out.
  4. Turn on two-step verification (Settings → Account). It stops someone stealing your account with the code sent by SMS. It isn't enough against a device being added: hence rule no. 2.

The message looks ordinary. It comes from a contact, it's polite, it asks for a gesture that costs nothing. And that's exactly why it works. I received it on 21 October. That same evening, two people in my family forwarded me almost the same one, this time for “Eliška” and “a free dance class”.

Rather than simply deleting it, I wanted to understand what was behind it. Here's what you find.

01How the scam works, in five steps

  1. Someone you know already got caught

    The message comes from a contact's real WhatsApp account. But it's no longer them writing: their account was stolen the same way, a few days earlier.

  2. A perfectly sweet request

    “Vote for my friends' daughter, it's important to her.” A child, a dream, one click: nobody wants to say no.

  3. A pretty contest page

    An elegant site, two little dancers, a tight vote counter (497 against 492). It's all fake: the school, the photos, the numbers.

  4. The trap: “confirm your vote with WhatsApp”

    To “prevent cheating”, the page mimics WhatsApp and asks for your number, then a code. That code is WhatsApp asking your permission to open your account on the hacker's computer.

  5. And it starts again, with your contacts

    The hacker reads your conversations and writes to your whole contact list using your name and your photo: “vote for Sophie”. Sometimes they also ask for money “urgently”.

02The code is the key to your house

WhatsApp lets you use your account on a computer (WhatsApp Web) or a tablet. To link a new device, there are two ways: scan a QR code, or receive a code to type on your phone. It's made for you, so you can read your messages on your PC.

The trap hijacks exactly this mechanism. When you type your number into the fake page, it's the hacker's server that asks WhatsApp: “I'd like to link a device to the account for this number”. WhatsApp, cautiously, sends a confirmation request to your phone. The fake page then tells you: “type this code to validate your vote”. You do it. You've just opened the door.

Real screenshots, taken in a sandbox (any.run) with a fake number. The page is in English here; it adapts to the visitor's language. It does send you to Linked devices: that is exactly the path the hacker takes to invite themselves into your account.

The image to remember

The code you're asked for has nothing to do with a vote: it's a copy of your keys. You would never give a copy of your keys to a stranger to “validate your entry” in a contest. Here, it's the same. With that copy, they walk into your home whenever they want, read your mail and talk to your loved ones while pretending to be you. And since you kept your own keys, you notice nothing.

03Why dancers, always?

Back in April, I'd already received this scam: “could you vote for Francesca? She's the daughter of my friends, the first prize is a free dance class”. In October, my family gets the same text, word for word, for “Eliška”. And me, a variant for “Sophie”. The theme doesn't change, because it's fearsomely well chosen:

  • A child: we drop our guard, and refusing seems petty.
  • A vote: a free, quick gesture, done without thinking. Nothing to pay, so nothing suspicious.
  • A friend asking: the message comes from an account we know. We don't distrust our loved ones.
  • “Preventing cheating”: the perfect excuse to ask for a login with WhatsApp. It even looks serious.

The ballet adds a chic, international touch: “the First International Ballet School in Prague”. This school really exists, and has nothing to do with any of this: the crooks steal its name to look credible. The fake page, for its part, exists in at least twelve languages.

Public screenshots taken by urlscan.io, a service that “visits” suspicious sites in our place. I entered nothing on these pages.

04How you investigate a scam like this one

A website is a bit like a shop. It has a sign (the name, balletday.click), an address (the server where it's hosted), an ID card (its security certificate, the little padlock) and a business register (who bought the name, and when). The crooks hide all of this. But there's almost always a loose thread sticking out.

Investigating means pulling on those threads, one by one. For this one, I used Bounce, an automated investigation tool I'm building: you give it a suspicious link, it follows the leads one at a time and draws the map of what it finds. What it reports then has to be re-read and verified, lead by lead.

Concretely, here's what happened on 21 October:

  1. 12:44

    I receive the message. The domain name balletday.click was bought one hour earlier. The crooks throw their sites away like tissues: a brand-new site has no bad reputation yet, so no antivirus blocks it.

  2. early afternoon

    I open the link in a disposable, monitored computer (a “sandbox”, any.run). There you see the page load a copy of the WhatsApp Web interface, then try to connect to WhatsApp with a number. The mechanism is confirmed.

  3. afternoon

    Bounce starts from the link and finds 27, then 32 twin sites: same page, same hidden path, same translated titles. And above all a server that wasn't hidden behind the mask, in Germany, hosting a dozen of these sites.

  4. 19:54

    My family forwards me the two “Eliška” messages. New link, 1forit21.shop. New Bounce investigation.

  5. 20:50

    Verdict: same campaign. Same page, same hidden link, and a second server that leaked, at the same host. Its certificate carries a telling name: fws2panel, most likely the crooks' control panel.

That last number struck me. In the page's code, there's a campaign number (181), a template number (27), a visit counter and advertising trackers. It's a factory: someone built a ready-to-use kit, with statistics, that gets rolled out into dozens of disposable sites. There are even “funnels”, intermediary sites paid to bring in victims.

06Already seen in April

This isn't the first time I've come across it. In April 2026, I'd already investigated with Bounce an almost identical message, received by me and then by someone close to me. At the time, we traced around twenty sites and a server hosted at a provider known for looking the other way, in Russia. I wrote a case study about it.

Same story, same sentence, six months later. But the sites, the servers and the technique have changed. Is it the same crew? Maybe. Or else the “kit” is rented out, and several crews use it. I lay out both hypotheses in the technical version. What is certain: it isn't stopping, and it'll come back with another first name.

07Did you click, or type a code?

The right moves, in order

  1. Open WhatsApp → Settings → Linked devices. Log out any device you don't recognise.
  2. Turn on two-step verification (Settings → Account) with a password you'll give to no one.
  3. Warn your contacts, by another channel (SMS, call, status): “if you got a voting message from me, it's not me, don't click”.
  4. If you can no longer get into your account: reinstall WhatsApp and re-register your number, the SMS code comes back to you. That logs out the intruder.
  5. Report the message in WhatsApp (long press → Report). If you sent money: call your bank straight away, then file a complaint. The single help point: 17cyber.gouv.fr (with the “account hacking” sheet from Cybermalveillance).

Share it

The best antivirus against this scam is for your loved ones to know about it before they receive it.

08What others say about it

I'm far from the only one to have seen it go by. This scam has been circulating in Europe since at least early 2025, each time with a different first name:

  1. January 2025 · Romania

    The national cybersecurity agency warns about “vote for Adeline”, with the lure of “a free study grant in France”. Then the hackers ask the victim's contacts for money.

  2. May 2025 · Czechia

    The police report “vote for Anna, my cousin”. At least five victims in a few days in a single district, each of whom sent the equivalent of more than €1,000 to a “friend”.

  3. September–October 2025 · Kaspersky, Bitdefender

    The antivirus vendors describe the same trap. Bitdefender counts 177 fake sites in two months, mostly in Poland, Romania and Germany.

  4. early 2026 · Italy

    The press calls it the “truffa della ballerina”. Many residents of the province of Reggio Emilia lose their WhatsApp account, including two mayors.

  5. March–May 2026 · Czechia, Switzerland

    The company Whalebone counts more than 200 domains in more than 15 countries. The Swiss national cybersecurity centre gives the example of “Sofia”.

  6. August–October 2026 · Austria, Spain

    The Austrian site Mimikama describes the fake “Prague ballet school” as early as August. And on 21 October, the day I get my message, Spain launches a national alert about… Eliska and Sofie.

What this investigation adds on top: the link with the April wave, the two servers hidden behind the sites and the lead on their “control panel”, and the funnel sites. And a version in French: I found no alert dedicated to this scam from the French authorities.

The detailed sources are listed at the bottom of the technical version.

TL;DR

  • Fake-vote campaign spread over WhatsApp, ballet theme, in the name of a real school in Prague. What the victim experiences is explained in the general-audience version; this one is about infrastructure and attribution.
  • Seed balletday[.]click, registered about one hour before receipt. Pivots on passive sources, with the CTI agent Bounce: 46 domains linked, kit dubbed “FIBS Prague Ballet Vote”.
  • Two off-CDN origins, both in ranges attributed to Dedik.io: 85.239.144[.]247 (AS207043) and 87.58.197[.]45 (AS62164). The certificate of the second names fws2panel[.]sbs.
  • Industrialised campaign: campaign number, template number, per-visitor statistics, build predating the domains, traffic distributors with affiliate parameters.
  • Link with the April 2026 wave: same lure text word for word, but no common infrastructure. Two hypotheses remain open: a single operator, or a kit rented to several crews.

01Context and method

On 21/10/2026, I receive the lure. The same evening, two relatives of mine receive a variant pointing to 1forit21[.]shop. The investigation relies on Bounce (code), a threat intelligence agent: it starts from an observable, chooses its pivots, builds the infrastructure graph and writes a report with its working hypothesis, its evidence and its self-criticism. Its pivots were steered by hand, cross-checked with the April wave and re-read one by one before being kept.

Rules of engagement

Passive and public sources: DNS, RDAP, Certificate Transparency, urlscan, VirusTotal, Shodan, Censys, passive DNS. Detonation in a sandbox (any.run, 22/10/2026)

I opened 1forit21[.]shop in a disposable browser (Edge on Windows 10) via any.run, entering a test number (+33 6 67 67 67 67), never a real account. any.run rates the task “Malicious activity” with the tags phishing, ip-check and websocket. The chain observed:

  1. https://1forit21[.]shop/: page “Audience voting — First International Ballet School in Prague”.
  2. A Cloudflare Turnstile iframe (challenges.cloudflare[.]com/cdn-cgi/challenge-platform/…/turnstile/…). Here, the anti-robot challenge serves as a screen: an automated scanner doesn't get through, a human does.
  3. https://1forit21[.]shop/cdn-cgi/phish-bypass?atok=…&original_path=%2F&cf-turnstile-response=…: the Turnstile token is handed back to the server, which then lets the visitor through. The /cdn-cgi/ path imitates Cloudflare's reserved prefix; its name (phish-bypass) is the kit's, not a Cloudflare feature to my knowledge.
  4. https://1forit21[.]shop/auth?return=%2F%3Fvoted%3D1&lang=en: page “Vote verification — FIBS Prague”.
  5. An iframe https://1forit21[.]shop/assets/autraizaion/?mode=phone&lng=en&return=… (spelling as displayed by any.run): it carries the number form, then the “Confirm verification” screen.

The length of the code is settled: 8 characters, in two blocks of 4 (XXXX-XXXX, here GANR-KPGE), capital letters in this capture. This is the format of the pairing code WhatsApp generates for “Link with phone number”: the page does not make up a voting code, it displays a pairing code and copies WhatsApp's steps (Linked devices → Link a device → Link with phone number). Limit: with a fake number, I cannot say whether this particular code would have been accepted by WhatsApp, and I typed no code. any.run also tags the task websocket: this is consistent with a server talking live to WhatsApp while the victim is on the page, without my having the content.

02Precedent: the April 2026 wave

In April, two near-identical lures (“vote for Francesca”) had already served me as seeds: konkurzfrance[.]click/home/votenam, then francevoice29[.]click/home/voteng. What the published case study said:

  • A “School votingFR” kit on /home/vote* paths. Domains in .click, .cfd, .shop and .club, registered between 02/04 and 24/05/2026, mostly at Dynadot (Global Domain Group) and Spaceship.
  • One unmasked origin, 217.145.226[.]186, at AS205775 (Neon Core Network), a bulletproof-type host.
  • The strongest grouping evidence was an origin certificate (CN voteingmap[.]click) whose SANs linked three campaign domains. The JARM, for its part, was Cloudflare's generic JARM and proved nothing.
  • About two dozen domains in three languages (FR, DE, IT), several of them at 0/94 on VirusTotal at scan time.

03What the page says about its authors

Without entering anything, the page itself is already a trove of fingerprints:

  • A platform, not a site. The page carries a campaign number (181), a template number (/p/27/), a per-recipient identifier in the URL (/l/<token>) and a visitor identifier for per-event statistics. The same template gets rolled out into campaigns, and each campaign is measured.
  • Built before the domains. The version parameter of the translation files dates the build to 19/10 at 04:09, two days before balletday[.]click was registered. The kit is stable; the domains are disposable.
  • Designed for the whole of Europe. The translation pack covers 42 languages. Public urlscan scans show titles in at least 12 languages (FR, DE, EN, ES, IT, NL, PT, FI, PL, NO, IS, TR). The default title is in Russian, which matches Whalebone's analysis of a kit written by Russian speakers. It's a clue, not an attribution.
  • Bought traffic on top of word of mouth. Meta and TikTok pixels, and traffic distributors (schoolbalet[.]club, kiddancebattle[.]web[.]id, baletkids[.]com) that redirect to the pages with sub_id_16/sub_id_17 parameters, the naming convention of the Keitaro tracker. It's the signature of an affiliate network.
  • A false pivot ruled out. The page also loads an advertising tracker from tk.mowell[.]tech. A urlscan search finds it on nearly 2,900 unrelated pages, mostly online shops: it's a shared advertising service, not a link between campaigns. It was excluded from the graph.

04Infrastructure: from the link to the two origins

The whole front is behind Cloudflare: anycast IP, *.ns.cloudflare.com name servers, and a generic JARM shared by more than a million hosts, hence worthless for grouping. Bounce “defuses” these nodes so as not to pivot on them, and looks for what sticks out: a misconfigured domain that reveals its real address, a shared certificate, a passive DNS history.

Two origins eventually appeared, and that's where the set overlaps:

Both IPs are in ranges registered in the name of the same host, Dedik.io, according to the European address registry (RIPE). The certificate of the second names fws2panel[.]sbs (“panel”), most likely the operators' administration interface; six domains of the kit resolved to it in the passive DNS history. An “anchor” domain, wafisher[.]xyz, links a subdomain to the first origin; its certificate logs expose subdomains named by module (wa, fb) and by operator, consistent with a multi-tenant panel. These readings of the names (fws2panel, wafisher) are interpretations, not facts confirmed by a public source.

Signs of anti-analysis

Without going into detail, two things make the campaign hard to block upstream:

  • Disposable domains. balletday[.]click was registered an hour before the message arrived; 1forit21[.]shop the day before. The names are mass-produced (1forit21/2forit21, fsk19hon2/fsk19hon3), and VirusTotal in fact tags one of them dga. Any reputation-based filter arrives after the first wave.
  • A page that stays silent depending on the visitor. The root of the domains serves an empty shell to bots; the kit only appears on the right token path. The ecosystem reacts all the same: Cloudflare put up its “Suspected Phishing” interstitial on some of the domains during the day, and some no longer resolve. The rotation continues on fresh names.

05Same wave, two investigations: the overlap

The two messages, received at different times and by different people, lead to the same campaign. Evidence, from strongest to weakest:

  • Same page, same token. 1forit21[.]shop serves the exact title “Public Vote — First International Dance School of Prague” and the same path /l/fibs2026e10 as balletday[.]click.
  • Domains common to both investigations: balletday[.]click, balethdancing[.]art, dancefibs[.]site, ihonajjdi[.]shop, softfranpro[.]cloud.
  • Same origin host (Dedik.io) for both unmasked IPs, and a certificate pointing to a common panel.
  • Weaker: a common but banal Cloudflare JARM, and a registration contact that is most likely a nominee.

06April versus October: same actor?

It's the most interesting question, and the honest answer is: undecidable as things stand. I compared the April wave and the October one term by term.

The theme and the text are identical; no technical fingerprint is. Between the two, we observe a continuous evolution of the kit's paths (from /home/vote up to /l/<token>) over several months. Two readings hold:

Hypothesis A — single operator

A single group evolving its kit and changing host over the months. For: the continuity of theme, text and registrar. Against: no shared infrastructure.

Hypothesis B — rented kit (PhaaS)

A single phishing “kit” sold or rented to several crews, who share it with their own domains and servers. For: the affiliate distributors, the industrialisation, the “phaas” label I'd already put on it in April.

I lean towards B, without settling it. What would tip the scale: an origin IP common to both waves, a shared certificate, or the real origin server of the October wave. Nothing of the kind is accessible through passive sources alone.

07Method notes

Three points that mattered in this investigation:

  • Defuse before pivoting. Pivoting on a shared Cloudflare JARM or IP makes you “find” thousands of imaginary links. A good part of the work consists of ruling out: the generic JARM, the shared advertising pixel, the cheap registrar.
  • A single leak is enough. The whole front is masked; it took just one misconfigured sibling domain to reveal an origin, and from there everything else.
  • Name your confidence level. “Same actor” is not a fact, it's a hypothesis, more or less likely. You have to say so as such.
