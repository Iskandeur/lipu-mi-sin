Everyone sees only one shadow

An opinion on a subject is the shadow of an object you never see whole: flat, partial, and drawn by the spot where you happen to stand. Other people see other shadows. Putting them together is how you guess the object. An idea to play with rather than believe, with what makes it solid and the places where it breaks.

Alexandre Pinoteau23.10.20263 interactive scenes

01A shadow doesn't lie, but it doesn't tell everything

An object, a lamp, a wall. The shadow invents nothing: every dark area matches a real piece of the object. But it has lost an entire dimension, and its shape depends as much on the angle of the light as on the object itself.

An opinion works the same way. On any subject (work, money, love, life in general), everyone sees the projection of an object bigger than themselves. The angle is what you've lived through: a country, a job, a family, wounds, books. It's not a flaw in reasoning, it's geometry. Nobody looks from nowhere.

02Two shadows that contradict each other

Ana looks at the floor and sees a disk. Ben looks at the wall and sees a square. Each has proof right in front of their eyes, and each can call the other blind for years.

Two reflexes are tempting, and a third one works. The first: decide who's right. The second: split the difference, which gives a limp, reasonable-looking shape that neither Ana nor Ben sees and that is wrong for both. The third: look for which object could cast both shadows at once. They were both right, and their contradiction was precisely the missing information: the trace of a depth that neither could see alone.

03Sculpting by subtraction

A shadow mostly tells you what the object is not: anything that would stick out of the shadow is ruled out. So you start from a block, each witness carves away what doesn't fit what they see, and what's left comes close to the object. In computer vision, this carved block is called the visual hull. A medical scanner does the same thing in finer detail, with X-rays taken all around the patient.

Your turn

The object is hidden. All you have is your own shadow (the yellow dot). Add witnesses by touching the ring: the rim is eye level, the center is a view from above. Try to guess the object before revealing it.

This isn't an average. A shadow doesn't vote, it constrains: it adds nothing, it eliminates possibilities.

Why the image holds up

  • The math is on its side. In 1917, Johann Radon showed that an object is entirely determined by its X-ray projections taken from every angle. That's the result CT scanners rest on (Nobel Prize 1979, Cormack and Hounsfield).
  • An X-ray beats a shadow. A shadow is binary; an X-ray tells you how much material the light went through. A nuanced opinion ("mostly", "rather", "except when") carries more information than a yes or a no, and helps more with reconstruction.
  • Diversity matters more than numbers. For a crowd estimating a figure, the collective error equals the average error of the individuals minus the diversity of their estimates (Scott Page's "diversity prediction theorem"). Distant angles are worth more than numerous ones.
  • It explains disagreement without calling anyone an idiot. Two honest, clear-headed people can contradict each other without either being wrong. That's a more charitable hypothesis than "the other one is stupid or acting in bad faith", and more often right.

04Where the image breaks

A metaphor you don't know how to attack isn't worth much. This one cracks in five places. The first three can be tested in the scene above.

1. A thousand neighbors are worth one witness

Ten neighbors gain you a few points of similarity. A single stranger often does better than all ten. What reconstructs isn't the number of opinions, it's the gap between the angles. A news feed, a circle of friends, a team often hand back the same shadow a hundred times. A hundred copies of the same shadow make you more sure of yourself, not more right.

2. Hollows cast no shadow

With object 3, similarity plateaus, however many witnesses you add. It's a mug, and its hollow sticks out of no silhouette, from any angle. Worse: a solid cylinder casts exactly the same shadows. You can gather every outside viewpoint and conclude, very confidently, that the object is something it isn't. Some things can only be seen from the inside: what it is to be poor, sick, exiled, a parent. Outside witnesses, however many, don't reconstruct them. You have to listen to the ones who are inside.

3. One liar is enough

Since we only keep what all the shadows allow, a liar carves into what's true, and nothing glues back what he removed. If you lower the confidence, you only keep what a majority confirms: the liar no longer weighs anything, but the object gets thicker, because you also throw away part of what the honest witnesses were teaching you. There's no universally good setting: between gullibility and deafness, everyone picks the price they pay.

4. You don't know where others are looking from, or where you are

In the scene, each witness's position is known to the degree. In life, you have to guess where the other is speaking from, and everyone knows their own angle poorly. Cryo-electron microscopy runs into the same problem: it reconstructs molecules from thousands of noisy images taken from unknown angles. It works, with a famous trap, "Einstein from noise": align images of pure noise on a portrait of Einstein, average them, and Einstein appears. Whoever reads other people's shadows through their own model reconstructs… their own model.

5. What if there were no object?

The whole image assumes an object out there, the same for everyone, that doesn't move while you look at it. For a question of fact (the climate, an accident, a disease), that's reasonable. For "life in general", it's a bet. Meaning, taste, the value of a life may not be objects we project, but things that gazes partly make. And a society changes when you look at it. Even the final reconstruction lives in a head, which has its own angle: there is no "view from nowhere". The image then holds as a discipline rather than a truth: act as if there were an object, so as to keep looking for it, without claiming to hold it.

05What you can keep from it

  1. Look for the angle that's farthest away, not the most common.
  2. Faced with a contradiction, ask "which object would make these two shadows?" before "who's wrong?".
  3. Ask where the other person is looking from, and where you are looking from.
  4. Give your reasons, not only your conclusion: an X-ray rather than a shadow.
  5. Tune your confidence: enough for every witness to teach you something, not so much that a single liar sculpts the whole thing.
  6. Remember the hollows: listen to those who see from the inside.

Already said, differently

  • Plato, the cave (The Republic, book VII): prisoners see only shadows. In Plato, salvation is getting out to see the object. Here, nobody gets out: there are only shadows, and we compare them.
  • The blind men and the elephant, an Indian parable: each touches one part and takes it for the whole. The Jains drew a doctrine from it, anekāntavāda, "non-one-sidedness".
  • The cover of Gödel, Escher, Bach (Hofstadter, 1979): a carved block whose shadows spell out a G, an E and a B.
  • Thomas Nagel, The View from Nowhere (1986): objectivity as an effort to detach from your own point of view, an effort that never reaches the end.

A sixth flaw? It's welcome: it's exactly the kind of shadow this text is missing.
