BrixHub: the search engine for your stolen data

A site takes your name and spits back your address, your phone number, your social security number. It says it indexes 14 billion records, taken from breaches. And it offers to remove yours… for a fee. It's targeted by the French justice system, and yet it's still online. I looked at how it holds up.

Alexandre Pinoteau21.10.2026with help from Bounce

Two articles, one investigation. You can switch between them at any time.

If you only read this

  1. Don't use it, not even out of curiosity, not even to look up your own name. Searching for someone else can expose you to prosecution, and the French justice system has referred this site to a prosecutor.
  2. Never pay to have your file removed, and never send your ID document. You'd be paying the people who hold your stolen data, and handing them one more piece.
  3. You can check your leaks for free and safely on haveibeenpwned.com, a legitimate service that displays no data.
  4. If your data is out there: change your important passwords, turn on two-factor authentication, and keep an eye on your bank accounts.

BrixHub is a search engine. But instead of web pages, it searches through data leaks: the files stolen during breaches of companies and government bodies. You type a name, a phone number, an email, and it returns a file on you. It claims more than 14 billion records.

In October 2026, the French press picks up on it and the Digital Affairs minister refers the matter to the justice system. Many people wonder what it is, and whether they can use it. The short answer: no, definitely not. Here's why, and how this kind of site works.

01Where does this data come from?

From real leaks. In recent years, plenty of French companies and bodies have had their customer files stolen: telecom operators, health insurers, retail sites, public services. Each leak means millions of people whose name, address, phone number or worse end up in the wild.

A site like BrixHub doesn't hack anything itself. It collects these scattered files, cross-references them, and makes them searchable with a single click. That's the whole problem: an isolated leak is a pain to exploit, but a search engine that links your name, your address, your phone number and your social security number on a single file is a weapon for harassment, identity theft and fraud.

Worth noting

BrixHub proudly displays the names of public bodies as sources. None has confirmed it, and I don't repeat these claims: they are its assertions, not established facts.

02The real business: selling you your own peace of mind

The most perverse part isn't the search. It's the “GDPR removal” option. The site offers, for a fee, to remove your file. Around a few euros for a handful of files, according to accounts. Sometimes, a “free” removal… in exchange for a photo of your ID document.

This is the trap within the trap

You'd be paying the people who hold your stolen data, so they'll promise to erase it. Nothing guarantees they do. And if you send your ID document, you give them one more piece of data, perfectly genuine this time. The deputy minister for Digital Affairs said so publicly on 5 October: “never pay to have your data removed” (AFP). So: don't pay, send nothing.

And this isn't just a theoretical warning. On X (formerly Twitter), users describe having paid to have their file removed: it did disappear… for a few days, before reappearing. No surprise: erasing a line from a display doesn't make the data vanish from the stolen file, which the operator keeps. They can put it back online, resell it, or make you pay again.

03Why isn't it shut down?

Because shutting down a site like this is far harder than it looks. BrixHub plays on three fronts:

  1. A screen

    The site hides behind a technical intermediary (Cloudflare) that masks the address of its real server. You see the façade, not the building.

  2. Disposable addresses

    It changes domain name as soon as one gets blocked: .fun, .net, .site, .org, .cc, then .to, .ru, .st… When one door closes, another is already open.

  3. Lenient countries

    It picks extensions run by small countries (São Tomé for .st, Tonga for .to) that don't monitor content and only act on a court order.

The result: you can't take it down with one click. What brings these sites down, as a rule, isn't closing the domain, it's arresting the people who run them. And that takes time.

04What the law says

I'm not a lawyer, but here's what comes out of the sources:

  • Looking at the site is not, in itself, an identified offence.
  • Searching for someone else, then keeping, sharing or using the result, does on the other hand expose you to real risks: receiving stolen goods, unfair collection of data, even harassment or impersonation if you target a person.
  • Running the site (those who operate it) amounts to fraudulent access to and extraction of data: several years in prison and heavy fines.

On 5 October 2026, the deputy minister for Digital Affairs referred the matter to the Paris prosecutor. Her advice, picked up everywhere: “never pay to have your data removed”.

You may be in their files. What to do?

  1. Don't go check on BrixHub. Use haveibeenpwned.com (enter your email, it tells you which known leaks it appears in).
  2. Change the passwords of the accounts concerned, and turn on two-factor authentication everywhere.
  3. Watch out for targeted scams: with this data, someone can reach you pretending to be your bank or a public service. When in doubt, hang up and call back the official number.
  4. If your file is online: keep a screenshot of your own file only as evidence, report it, and refer the matter to the CNIL if nothing happens. Don't pay.

01Context and method

BrixHub is a “data leak search engine” in French. It indexes databases from compromises and offers a name-based search, plus a paid file removal. It has been targeted in France by a criminal referral since 05/10/2026. The starting question was simple: can you use its API? This investigation answers it.

Rules of engagement

Public and passive sources: WHOIS/RDAP from the registries, DNS, Certificate Transparency, urlscan.io, VirusTotal, Shodan/InternetDB, passive DNS, press. First pass with the CTI agent Bounce, completed by hand. I never connected to the service, I ran no search on it, and I bypassed no protection (the page returns a Cloudflare 403 challenge to a client without a browser, not bypassed). Defanged domains ([.]).

02What the service claims

According to its own pages and the press (figures to be taken as claims, not facts):

14.5 bnrecords claimed (counter on the home page)
~€5to remove 10 files, according to accounts
€10→90subscriptions, from “per month” to “lifetime”
12+successive domains since February 2026

Search by name, email, phone number, Discord identifier; data advertised up to the French social security number (NIR) and IBAN; coverage of France, Belgium, Switzerland. A “family tree” tab, an “open API, no key, rate-limited by IP” (this is the answer to the original question: it advertises an API, and using it amounts to knowingly extracting stolen data), a Telegram bot and an X account. Legal notices mentioning a “company based in Russia”, with no name. None of these claims is verifiable from the outside, and I don't take them as given.

03Infrastructure: a façade, no building

The whole web side is behind Cloudflare, on the three active domains. The origin has never leaked. The shared fingerprints confirm a single operator:

Three markers link the active domains: the same pair of Cloudflare name servers (irena / weston), the same SPF policy (v=spf1 mx:brixhub[.]to -all) and the same page title. The .st was born on 30/09, five days before the minister's referral.

The only leak: the mail server

The web origin is masked, but the mail isn't. The three domains delegate their mail (MX and SPF) to mail.brixhub[.]to, which resolves to an IP outside Cloudflare:

mail.brixhub[.]to → 195.58.146[.]225 AS9009 M247 · ptr mail.brixhub.to Postfix + OpenSSH 9.6p1 · Ubuntu 24.04 self-signed SMTP cert, CN=mail.brixhub.to (04/10/2026, valid 10 years) no other domain observed on this IP in passive DNS

It's the only piece of infrastructure proper to the operator that the investigation exposed. It doesn't “unmask” the website (hosted elsewhere, behind Cloudflare), but it's a solid pivot: a dedicated IP, a named host, a dated certificate. Bounce itself flagged it in its self-criticism: this “mail” IP was the most interesting enrichment target, and the weak point of the operator's configuration.

04Why it stays online

BrixHub's resilience is no accident. It rests on four choices:

  • A Cloudflare front that masks the real host and absorbs abuse reports.
  • A domain rotation across varied TLDs. The classic gTLDs (.fun, .cc, .site, .org) were suspended by their registrars between June and August; the operator migrated each time to a more cooperative ccTLD.
  • Low-cooperation registries. The .st registry (São Tomé, Swedish back-end) states in its policy that it “does not monitor content” and only cancels a domain on the decision of a competent court. The .to (Tonga, Tonic operator) has the same reputation, even though it has already suspended domains on a court order.
  • A fallback channel. A Telegram bot announces the new address at each switch, and the mail stays anchored on .to whatever happens. The service survives the loss of any one of its domains.

In other words: it's not the seizure of a domain that brings these services down, it's the apprehension of the operators. The precedents show it: WeLeakInfo (seized in 2020), RaidForums (2022), the BreachForums administrators (arrested in France in 2025), and several French “lookups” dismantled in 2026 with arrests. Every time, the judicial investigation, not the technical blocking.

05Is it legal? Can you use their API?

No, and no. Legal summary (based on lawyers' analyses and the press; I'm not a lawyer):

SideWhat's at stake
OperatorFraudulent access to and extraction of data (art. 323-1 et seq. of the penal code), up to several years in prison and heavy fines.
UserConsulting is not an identified offence in itself. But searching for a third party and then holding, sharing or using the result exposes you to receiving stolen goods (art. 321-1), unfair collection (226-18), even harassment or impersonation (223-1-1). Paying or sending your ID document aggravates the exposure.
APIAutomated access to a database of stolen data is deliberate extraction at scale. And it funds an extortion service. To be avoided.

The Court of Cassation did in fact rule in February 2026 that intangible information can be the object of receiving stolen goods. The case concerned trade secrets, not data leaks, but the principle undermines the idea that “using” this data would be risk-free.

To check your own leaks, without funding or enriching anyone: Have I Been Pwned. It tells you which known leaks your email appears in, and never displays the data itself. It's the legal counter-example to BrixHub.

06What the investigation does not settle

For the sake of honesty, the limits:

  • The site's real host and the identity of the operators: masked. Nothing attributable through passive sources alone.
  • The real origin of the data: the site displays prestigious sources (public bodies), none confirmed. I don't repeat these claims.
  • The exact amounts and the real existence of the API and the subscriptions rest on second-hand accounts. Same for the files paid for then reappeared: these are accounts from users on X, consistent with how the service works, but which I couldn't verify one by one.
  • Several press articles (franceinfo, Le Monde, Siècle Digital…) could not be read in full, only through their titles and excerpts.
